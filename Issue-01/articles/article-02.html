THE UNSPOKEN OCEAN

Securing Tamil Nadu's Domestic Water Future Through Renewable Offshore Infrastructure

THE UNSAID

Water scarcity is often described as a shortage of freshwater. Perhaps it is also a shortage of perspective. Every year, we wait for the monsoon. We celebrate when reservoirs fill and worry when they do not. We drill deeper borewells, transport water over longer distances and compete for increasingly stressed freshwater resources.

Yet, along Tamil Nadu's 1,076-kilometre coastline, the world's largest water reservoir touches our land every single day. The ocean has never lacked water. What we have lacked is a sustainable way to transform that water into a reliable public resource. The question before us is no longer: "Will it rain enough?" It is becoming: "Can renewable energy help us secure the water we need, while protecting the resources we already have?" Sometimes, the greatest opportunity is not discovering a new resource. It is seeing an existing one differently.

IN ONE NUMBER

108

Based on the engineering assumptions presented in this research note, approximately 108 strategically located Integrated Coastal Water Security Centres (100 MLD each) could theoretically produce about 10,800 MLD of freshwater—equivalent to the domestic water planning demand for a population of approximately 80 million people using a planning norm of 135 litres per person per day. The number is not a construction target. It is a way of understanding the scale of the opportunity.

WHY IT MATTERS

Water security is no longer just about finding more water. It is about building a system that remains reliable during droughts, population growth and climate change. Tamil Nadu has always depended on many water sources—rainfall, rivers, reservoirs, groundwater, lakes and tanks. Each remains essential. This research note explores how renewable-powered desalination could become one additional pillar of a diversified water portfolio. If developed responsibly, such a system could:

  • Supplement domestic water supplies.
  • Reduce pressure on stressed groundwater.
  • Improve drought resilience.
  • Enable cleaner water production using renewable energy.
  • Create opportunities for resource recovery and circular economy practices.

The aim is not to replace nature. It is to work with it more intelligently.

PAST HISTORY

Tamil Nadu's history is a story of water wisdom. Long before modern pipelines and pumping stations, communities built interconnected tanks, lakes, canals and reservoirs that harvested every possible drop of rain. These systems supported agriculture, settlements and ecosystems for centuries because they reflected a simple principle: Store water when nature provides it. Use it carefully when it does not.

Today's challenges are different. The population has grown. Cities have expanded. Climate patterns have become more variable. Groundwater is under increasing pressure in many regions. Yet the lesson from history remains unchanged. Every generation must adapt its water systems to the realities of its time. Yesterday's answer was the village tank. Tomorrow's answer may include the ocean—used responsibly, powered by renewable energy, and integrated with every other water source.

LEGACY FOR CONTINUITY

Future generations should inherit more than pipelines and treatment plants. They should inherit confidence. Confidence that safe water will be available. Confidence that renewable energy will power essential public services. Confidence that engineering can strengthen, rather than weaken, our relationship with nature. If every litre of water is produced with care, every unit of energy comes from renewable sources, and every resource is recovered wherever practical, then water infrastructure becomes more than a utility. It becomes a legacy. The question is therefore not whether we can build such a future. The question is whether we are willing to begin.

Opening Thought

Nature teaches us that resilience never comes from dependence on a single source. It comes from many systems working together.

Water Security = Conservation + Diversification + Renewable Innovation

THE UNSPOKEN OCEAN

From Domestic Water Deficit to Water Security

How Renewable Offshore Wind and the Ocean Can Help Secure Tamil Nadu’s Domestic Water Future

“Water scarcity is not always the absence of water. Sometimes it is the absence of a long-term plan.”

THE UNSAID

Every summer, the same story unfolds. Reservoirs shrink. Groundwater levels fall. Cities worry about water shortages. Villages wait for tanker lorries. Farmers anxiously watch the skies.

Then, within a few months, the northeast monsoon arrives. Rivers overflow, reservoirs fill, urban streets flood and enormous quantities of freshwater eventually flow back into the sea. Tamil Nadu experiences both water scarcity and water abundance—often within the same year. The question therefore is not whether water exists. The question is whether we are managing every available source wisely.

Along Tamil Nadu’s 1,076-kilometre coastline lies the world’s largest water reservoir. For generations, we have looked at the ocean as the end of our rivers. Perhaps it is time to begin seeing it as the beginning of a new chapter in water security.

IN ONE NUMBER

108

Using a planning scenario based on an estimated population of 80 million people and a domestic water planning norm of 135 litres per person per day, Tamil Nadu’s theoretical domestic water requirement is approximately 10,800 million litres per day (MLD). This quantity could theoretically be produced through 108 strategically distributed 100 MLD Integrated Coastal Water Security Centres, developed gradually over the next two decades as part of a long-term water security strategy. The number is not a construction target. It is a way of understanding the scale of the opportunity.

WHY IT MATTERS

Water is no longer only an environmental issue. It is a public health issue. An economic issue. An agricultural issue. An industrial issue. A climate resilience issue.

Tamil Nadu has always depended on rainfall, rivers, reservoirs, lakes, groundwater and traditional tanks. These sources must remain the foundation of the state’s water security. This research note explores an additional opportunity: Using renewable offshore wind energy to convert seawater into reliable domestic water while reducing pressure on natural freshwater resources. The objective is not to replace nature. The objective is to protect it.

A LESSON FROM HISTORY

Tamil Nadu has one of the richest water management traditions in the world. Our ancestors constructed thousands of irrigation tanks, temple ponds, lakes and interconnected canals. Every drop of rain was considered valuable. Their philosophy was simple: Store water when nature provides it. Use it carefully when nature does not.

Today’s challenges are different. Population has increased. Cities have expanded. Groundwater extraction has intensified. Climate variability has increased. Yet the principle remains unchanged. Every generation must strengthen its water system before passing it to the next.

TAMIL NADU’S WATER PORTFOLIO

Water security cannot depend upon a single source. A resilient future requires an integrated portfolio consisting of:

  • Rainfall harvesting
  • Rivers
  • Reservoirs
  • Lakes and village tanks
  • Groundwater recharge
  • Treated wastewater reuse
  • Smart water management
  • Renewable-powered desalination

Each source performs a different role. Together they create resilience.

A NEW ENGINEERING VISION

Instead of constructing only a few very large desalination plants, this research proposes a distributed network of Integrated Coastal Water Security Centres (ICWSCs). Each centre would include:

  • 100 MLD seawater reverse osmosis desalination
  • Dedicated offshore wind turbines
  • Renewable electricity integration
  • Battery energy storage
  • Emergency water storage
  • Smart monitoring and automation
  • Water quality laboratory
  • Future-ready resource recovery systems

Each centre becomes not merely a desalination plant, but a regional public water infrastructure hub.

WHY 100 MLD?

While larger plants can benefit from economies of scale, strategically distributed 100 MLD centres offer significant advantages for a statewide domestic water system. Water can be produced closer to regional demand. Transmission distances are reduced. Pumping requirements decrease. Maintenance becomes easier. Future expansion can occur in phases. Regional resilience improves because the state does not depend upon only a few large facilities. Water security is therefore determined not by the size of a plant, but by how intelligently water is produced, distributed and made available to every community.

POWERING WATER WITH THE WIND

Each 100 MLD Integrated Coastal Water Security Centre requires approximately 146 GWh of electricity annually for desalination and auxiliary systems. Three dedicated 15 MW offshore wind turbines, operating at a 50 percent capacity factor, can generate approximately 197 GWh of renewable electricity each year, providing sufficient energy for plant operation while creating an operational energy margin. Instead of burning coal to produce drinking water, renewable energy becomes the engine of water security.

THINKING LIKE NATURE

The purpose of desalination is not to replace rainfall. It is to protect the value of rainfall. Rainwater should continue to recharge groundwater, fill reservoirs, sustain rivers and support agriculture. Renewable desalination provides a dependable supplementary source for domestic water, allowing precious freshwater resources to be managed more strategically. Nature never depends upon one source alone. Neither should we.

CIRCULAR ECONOMY AT THE COAST

Every Integrated Coastal Water Security Centre can be designed using circular economy principles. Freshwater becomes the primary product. Where technically and economically feasible, valuable resources can also be recovered from concentrated brine, while process integration minimises waste and improves overall resource efficiency. The long-term objective is simple: Produce more. Waste less. Recover wherever practical. The most sustainable desalination facility is not the one that produces the most water. It is the one that leaves the least behind.

WILL THE OCEAN RUN DRY?

This is perhaps the most common question. The answer is no. A 100 MLD desalination plant requires approximately 200 MLD of seawater, assuming a 50 percent recovery rate. Even if 108 such centres were operating, the annual seawater intake would represent an extraordinarily small fraction of the world’s oceans. The engineering challenge is therefore not the quantity of seawater available. It is ensuring environmentally responsible intake systems, careful management of concentrated brine, protection of marine ecosystems and continuous environmental monitoring. The ocean is not a finite reservoir like a lake. It is part of Earth’s dynamic and continuously circulating water system.

A TWENTY-YEAR ROADMAP

This vision does not suggest constructing all facilities immediately. It proposes a phased programme.

  • Phase I would strengthen existing coastal urban regions.
  • Phase II would establish regional coastal hubs.
  • Phase III would extend reliable domestic water to inland districts.
  • Phase IV would integrate advanced resource recovery, smart monitoring and renewable energy optimisation.
  • Every completed phase strengthens the next.

THE LEGACY FOR CONTINUITY

Imagine a Tamil Nadu where every village has reliable domestic water. Groundwater is no longer over-extracted. Rainwater harvesting is universal. Traditional tanks and reservoirs are restored. Agriculture benefits from protected freshwater resources. Cities receive dependable water through renewable-powered coastal infrastructure. The question is not whether such a future is technically possible. The engineering pathway exists. The renewable resources exist. The coastline exists. The ocean has always been there. The real question is whether we have the collective vision to begin.

CLOSING THOUGHT

Water security will never be achieved by depending on a single source. It will be achieved by respecting every source, protecting freshwater, embracing renewable innovation and ensuring that today’s decisions strengthen tomorrow’s resilience. The ocean has never lacked water. Perhaps what has been missing is our willingness to listen to what it has always been quietly offering.

BEYOND SUSTAINABILITY – THE CONTINUITY

Every Drop Secured. Every Tonne of Carbon Avoided. Every Generation Protected.

There are moments in history when engineering does more than solve a technical problem. It changes the future of a society. Water security is one such moment.

For decades, Tamil Nadu has depended on rainfall, rivers, reservoirs and groundwater to sustain its people. These natural resources have served generations with remarkable resilience. They must continue to remain the foundation of our water future.

But climate change is altering the way we experience water. Rainfall is becoming increasingly variable. Population continues to grow. Groundwater is under increasing stress in many regions. The challenge before us is therefore not simply to produce more water. It is to protect every source of freshwater while creating a dependable, climate-resilient supplementary source for generations to come.

That is where renewable-powered Integrated Coastal Water Security Centres can become transformative. This research note demonstrates a conceptual engineering pathway in which Tamil Nadu’s domestic water planning demand could be supported through strategically distributed renewable desalination infrastructure powered by offshore wind energy. The significance lies not only in the water produced, but in the wider environmental and societal benefits.

Engineering Perspective (Illustrative Planning Scenario)

  • Domestic Water Planning Demand: 10,800 MLD
  • Annual Domestic Water Requirement: 3,942 GL
  • Integrated Coastal Water Security Centres: 108
  • Capacity of Each Centre: 100 MLD
  • Offshore Wind Turbines: 324 × 15 MW
  • Installed Renewable Capacity: 4.86 GW
  • Renewable Electricity for Water Production: 15.77 TWh per year
  • Illustrative Coal Consumption Avoided: ≈7.88 million tonnes per year
  • Illustrative CO₂ Emissions Avoided: ≈14.19 million tonnes per year

These numbers represent more than engineering calculations. They represent cleaner air. Reduced dependence on fossil fuels. Less pressure on groundwater. Greater resilience during droughts. A stronger and more reliable public water system.

Yet the greatest contribution may not be measured in megawatts or million litres. It is measured in what this approach allows us to preserve.

  • Rainwater can continue to recharge aquifers.
  • Reservoirs and lakes can be managed as strategic freshwater reserves.
  • Traditional tanks can regain their role in local water security.
  • Freshwater can increasingly be prioritised for agriculture, ecosystems and future generations.

At the same time, renewable energy transforms the ocean into a dependable supplementary source for domestic water. This is not a choice between conservation and desalination. It is the intelligent integration of both. Every Integrated Coastal Water Security Centre should also embrace Circular Economy and Zero Liquid Discharge principles wherever technically and economically feasible, ensuring that freshwater production, renewable energy integration and responsible resource recovery work together to minimise environmental impact.

Sustainability asks us to meet today’s needs without compromising tomorrow. Continuity asks an even bigger question. What kind of Tamil Nadu will we leave behind? A state that reacts to every drought? Or a state that prepares decades in advance? History will not remember us merely for the infrastructure we built. It will remember whether we had the foresight to protect our natural resources, embrace renewable innovation and secure water for those who come after us.

THE UNSAID

The true measure of progress is not how much water we consume. It is how wisely we protect nature while ensuring that every future generation inherits a more secure, more resilient and more sustainable Tamil Nadu.

FROM TAMIL NADU TO INDIA

A Scalable Vision for National Water Security

This research note began with one simple question: Can renewable offshore energy help secure Tamil Nadu's domestic water future? The engineering assessment suggests that a technically feasible pathway exists. If implemented in carefully planned phases, renewable-powered Integrated Coastal Water Security Centres (ICWSCs) could become an important supplementary source of freshwater while reducing dependence on groundwater and fossil-fuel-based electricity.

Yet the opportunity extends far beyond Tamil Nadu. India possesses a coastline of approximately 11,100 kilometres, stretching across the Arabian Sea, the Bay of Bengal and the Indian Ocean. This coastline is more than a geographical boundary. It is a strategic national asset. Every coastal state possesses the opportunity to evaluate how renewable energy, desalination, water reuse and circular resource recovery can complement existing freshwater resources. The objective is not to replace rivers, reservoirs, groundwater or rainwater harvesting. The objective is to strengthen them.

A NATIONAL WATER RESILIENCE FRAMEWORK

Imagining India twenty years from now.

  • Every village continues to harvest rainwater.
  • Every lake and reservoir is protected.
  • Every river is restored.
  • Groundwater is managed sustainably.
  • Treated wastewater is reused wherever practical.
  • Strategically located coastal water security centres provide dependable supplementary supplies powered by renewable energy.

During droughts, communities remain resilient. During floods, more rainwater is harvested and stored. The nation becomes less vulnerable to rainfall variability because water security is no longer dependent on a single source. Instead, it is built upon a diversified and integrated water portfolio.
