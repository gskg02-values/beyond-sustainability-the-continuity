From Water Scarcity to Water Security

THE UNSPOKEN OCEAN

How Three Renewable-Powered Desalination Plants Could Redefine Tamil Nadu’s Drinking Water Future

THE UNSAID

For decades, Tamil Nadu has searched for water beneath the ground. We deepened borewells. We built reservoirs. We harvested rainwater. We restored lakes and tanks. Every drop mattered because water scarcity became part of our planning.

Yet, throughout this journey, one resource silently surrounded us every day. The ocean. Stretching for nearly 1,076 kilometres, Tamil Nadu’s coastline borders one of the world’s largest and most dependable water sources. While the sea cannot be consumed directly, modern desalination technology combined with renewable offshore wind energy now makes it technically possible to transform seawater into safe drinking water without depending on fossil fuels. Perhaps the greatest opportunity before Tamil Nadu is not discovering a new water source—but learning to use the one that has always existed.

IN ONE NUMBER

87.6 Billion Litres

Assuming a population of approximately 80 million people and a drinking water requirement of 3 litres per person per day, Tamil Nadu requires approximately: 87.6 billion litres of safe drinking water every year That equals: 87.6 GL per year

Unlike domestic water requirements for washing, sanitation and other household uses, the quantity of water required purely for drinking is surprisingly modest. This observation changes the scale of the challenge. The question therefore becomes: Can renewable energy continuously produce this quantity of drinking water from the ocean?

WHY IT MATTERS

Climate change is increasing rainfall variability. Groundwater tables continue to decline in many regions. Surface reservoirs remain dependent on seasonal monsoons. Population and urbanisation continue to increase. These challenges are often discussed independently. But they all point toward one common question: How can Tamil Nadu secure a permanent source of safe drinking water that is independent of rainfall? The ocean provides one possible answer.

RETHINKING WATER SECURITY

Traditionally, drinking water planning begins with rainfall. Rain fills reservoirs. Reservoirs recharge rivers. Rivers recharge groundwater. Groundwater supplies towns and villages. This system has served society for generations, but it remains vulnerable to prolonged droughts and changing climatic patterns. The ocean offers something fundamentally different. It is available every day. Every season. Every year. The challenge is no longer the availability of water. The challenge is sustainable energy.

THE OPPORTUNITY BEFORE TAMIL NADU

Imagine establishing three strategically located coastal renewable water centres. Each centre consists of:

  • One 100 MLD seawater desalination plant
  • Dedicated offshore wind turbines
  • Subsea power cables
  • Water storage reservoirs
  • Smart monitoring systems
  • Mineral recovery facilities
  • Future-ready hydrogen integration

Each facility functions independently while contributing to a statewide drinking water network. Instead of transporting fossil fuels to produce water, renewable energy harvested offshore becomes the primary source of power.

THE ENGINEERING PERSPECTIVE

Using modern reverse osmosis technology, one 100 MLD desalination plant can produce approximately: 36.5 GL of drinking water every year

Therefore: Three plants produce approximately: 109.5 GL/year

Estimated statewide drinking water requirement: 87.6 GL/year

This provides an operational reserve of approximately: 21.9 GL/year or 60 MLD This reserve could support maintenance shutdowns, emergency response, seasonal demand fluctuations and future population growth.

POWERING WATER WITH THE WIND

Water security should not create another environmental challenge. If desalination depends entirely on fossil-fuel electricity, drinking water security comes at the cost of higher greenhouse gas emissions. Instead, renewable offshore wind can become the dedicated energy source. Using the engineering assumptions presented in this research note: Each 100 MLD desalination plant requires approximately: 127.8 GWh of electricity annually Planning for three 15 MW offshore wind turbines per plant provides a practical renewable energy concept with operational margin. For three desalination plants:

  • Total offshore turbines: 9
  • Installed offshore capacity: 135 MW

This illustrates a future in which drinking water is produced using the energy of the wind rather than the combustion of coal.

FROM CARBON TO CONTINUITY

Renewable-powered desalination creates benefits beyond freshwater production. Illustrative engineering calculations indicate that replacing coal-generated electricity with offshore wind for these three plants could avoid approximately:

  • 345,000 tonnes of CO₂ emissions every year
  • 191,700 tonnes of coal consumption annually

This means that every litre of drinking water produced through renewable energy also contributes to reducing the environmental footprint of water infrastructure. Water security and climate action become part of the same solution.

A NEW WATER PORTFOLIO

Future water security should never depend upon a single source. Instead, resilience is created through diversity. Tamil Nadu’s future water portfolio could include:

  • Rainwater Harvesting
  • Rivers
  • Reservoirs
  • Lakes and Irrigation Tanks
  • Groundwater
  • Water Reuse
  • Renewable-Powered Desalination
  • Smart Water Management

Each source strengthens the others. The ocean does not replace existing water resources. It strengthens the entire portfolio by providing a dependable drinking water reserve.

THE UNSPOKEN ADVANTAGE

Unlike Ocean, the groundwater cannot be over-extracted in the conventional sense for drinking water production at these scales. Unlike rainfall, it does not depend upon monsoon variability. Unlike reservoirs, it does not empty after consecutive drought years. When powered by renewable energy and managed responsibly with sound environmental safeguards, desalination becomes a strategic resilience asset rather than merely another water project. The coastline itself becomes an opportunity for long-term water security.

BEYOND SUSTAINABILITY

Sustainability asks: “Can we continue using today’s resources responsibly?” Continuity asks a different question: “Can future generations depend upon this system regardless of changing climate?” Renewable-powered desalination represents more than technology. It represents resilience. It represents preparedness. It represents continuity. Every drop produced from the ocean using renewable energy reduces dependence on uncertain rainfall while preserving groundwater for future generations.

THE LEGACY QUESTION

If Tamil Nadu can transform an abundant ocean into a dependable source of clean drinking water using renewable energy, then the question is no longer whether water security is technically possible. The real question is whether we are ready to think differently. Because sometimes the greatest solution has never been hidden. It has simply remained… The Unspoken Ocean.

Every Drop Can Protect More Than Water.

Producing drinking water through renewable-powered desalination is not simply about meeting human needs. It is about changing the way we think about water infrastructure.

The engineering concept presented in this research note illustrates that approximately three 100 MLD desalination plants, powered by dedicated offshore wind turbines, could theoretically produce more than Tamil Nadu’s estimated annual drinking water requirement while reducing dependence on conventional fossil-fuel electricity.

Illustrative engineering calculations indicate that such a system could also contribute to:

  • Approximately 345,000 tonnes of CO₂ emissions avoided annually (relative to a coal-powered electricity baseline)
  • Approximately 191,700 tonnes of coal consumption avoided every year
  • Protection of groundwater reserves through reduced extraction for drinking water
  • Greater resilience against droughts and rainfall variability

The significance extends beyond these numbers. It demonstrates that one carefully designed infrastructure system can simultaneously contribute to water security, climate action and environmental stewardship.

The ocean becomes more than a water source. Renewable energy becomes more than electricity. Together, they become a long-term resilience system.

The goal is not to replace rainwater harvesting, reservoirs, rivers, lakes or groundwater. The goal is to strengthen them. When every water source works together, Tamil Nadu becomes more resilient to an uncertain climate.

That is the difference between solving today’s crisis and preparing for tomorrow’s challenges.
