THE FOOD WE GROW IS NOT YET THE FOOD WE SECURE

Tamil Nadu Has Production. The Next Mission Is Continuity.

Tamil Nadu does not have to begin its food-security journey from zero. It already grows rice, pulses, fruits, vegetables and a wide range of crops. Its farmers produce millions of tonnes of food every year. The Public Distribution System, procurement networks and agricultural infrastructure provide another layer of security.

So the question is not: “Can Tamil Nadu produce food?”
It clearly can.

The more important question for the next decade is: “How much of what we produce can we reliably protect, preserve and deliver when climate extremes arrive?” That is the real food-security challenge.

FROM PRODUCTION TO FOOD SECURITY

A tonne harvested is not automatically a tonne available to society. Between the field and the plate are many possible points of failure:

  • Harvest
  • Drying
  • Storage
  • Processing
  • Transport
  • Market
  • Household

A sudden downpour can damage a harvested crop. Excess moisture can spoil stored grain. A bumper vegetable harvest can collapse farm-gate prices when processing and cold-chain capacity cannot absorb the temporary surplus. A flood can destroy roads and interrupt movement. A heatwave can reduce production before harvest. A drought can reduce the next season’s planting.

Therefore, measuring food security only by annual production can give us a misleading sense of comfort.

PRODUCTION IS THE BEGINNING OF FOOD SECURITY — NOT THE END.

THE NET FOOD QUESTION

For tomorrow, perhaps Tamil Nadu needs a new agricultural metric:

GROSS PRODUCTION
minus POST-HARVEST LOSS
minus STORAGE LOSS
minus PROCESSING LOSS
minus TRANSPORT LOSS
equals NET FOOD SECURED

And even that is not enough. A resilient state must also maintain: Strategic stocks + emergency reserves + seed reserves + diversified crops + reliable water + alternative supply routes.

Because food security is not tested during a normal monsoon. It is tested when the monsoon fails.

THE PARADOX OF ABUNDANCE

There is a striking contradiction in agriculture. At one end, farmers struggle to produce food under heat, drought and uncertain rainfall. At the other end, perfectly usable fruits and vegetables can become economically worthless during seasonal gluts because markets, storage, processing or transport cannot absorb them quickly enough.

This is not necessarily a production failure. It is a systems failure.

The answer is not simply to produce less. It is to build the ability to: store more, process more, preserve more and waste less.

A tomato that cannot be sold today could become a product that remains available months later. A surplus fruit harvest could become pulp, dried fruit, juice or other shelf-stable products. A grain harvested during a good year could become part of a strategic reserve rather than becoming vulnerable to moisture and deterioration.

THE HARVEST OF TODAY CAN BECOME THE FOOD SECURITY OF TOMORROW.

CLIMATE CHANGES THE QUESTION

Tamil Nadu receives substantial rainfall. But rainfall is not the same as water security. Water arriving in a short, intense event may cause flooding. The same annual rainfall distributed differently could support agriculture.

The challenge is therefore not simply: How much rain do we receive?
It is: How much rain can we capture, recharge, store and make available to crops at the right time?

Tamil Nadu’s thousands of tanks, reservoirs, canals, groundwater systems and traditional water structures are therefore not merely irrigation infrastructure. They are food-security infrastructure.

Restoring them, modernising them and connecting water management with cropping decisions could be one of the state’s most important climate-resilience investments.

LAND IS ANOTHER RESERVE

Tamil Nadu has substantial areas classified as current fallow, other fallow and culturable waste. But we should not call all of this “unused agricultural land.”

  • Some land may lack water.
  • Some may be degraded.
  • Some may be economically unviable.
  • Some may have ecological importance and should never be cultivated.

The right question is: How much can be responsibly restored?

If even a carefully selected portion can be brought back into productive use through soil restoration, water harvesting, appropriate crops and climate-resilient farming, it becomes another layer of food-security capacity. Not by conquering nature. By working with it.

SELF-RELIANCE DOES NOT MEAN GROWING EVERYTHING

A self-reliant Tamil Nadu does not need to produce every food commodity within its borders. That would neither be practical nor necessarily sustainable.

Self-reliance should mean something more intelligent:

  • Be strong where we can be strong.
  • Diversify where we are vulnerable.
  • Reduce excessive dependence on strategically important commodities.
  • Maintain reserves for disruption.
  • Build the ability to recover quickly after a shock.

Rice and several horticultural crops provide a strong production foundation. Pulses offer room for greater domestic resilience. Edible oils reveal a significant dependence gap. Cold chains and food processing offer enormous opportunity. Water remains a foundational constraint.

The objective is therefore not 100% isolation. It is 100% preparedness for disruption.

FROM PESTICIDE DEPENDENCE TO SYSTEM HEALTH

Food security must also ask another question: What kind of agriculture are we securing? A system that produces food by progressively degrading soil, contaminating water and undermining biodiversity cannot remain secure indefinitely.

The goal should be safer, science-based agriculture: healthy soils + crop diversity + integrated pest management + biological controls + efficient water use + responsible chemical intervention where necessary.

The objective is not to replace science with slogans. It is to use less harmful intervention wherever nature and good agricultural practice can do the work.

THE HEALTH OF THE FARM IS PART OF THE SECURITY OF THE FOOD.

THE 8.5-CRORE QUESTION

Tamil Nadu’s population is projected to remain around 7.8 crore in the coming decades. But let’s deliberately ask a harder question. What if the state eventually had to secure food for 8.5 crore people?

And what if that larger population faced:

  • more heatwaves
  • irregular monsoons
  • intense rainfall
  • droughts
  • water stress
  • crop disease
  • disrupted transport
  • storage losses?

Would annual production alone be enough? Probably not.

But a food system designed around:

  • resilient production
  • water security
  • crop diversity
  • lower losses
  • processing
  • climate-resilient storage
  • strategic reserves
  • multiple supply routes

would be considerably stronger. That is the difference between food production and food continuity.

THE NEW FOOD SECURITY EQUATION

FOOD SECURITY = PRODUCTIVE LAND + RELIABLE WATER + RESILIENT CROPS + SAFE AGRICULTURE + PROTECTED HARVEST + PROCESSING + STORAGE + LOWER LOSS + STRATEGIC RESERVES + DISTRIBUTION THAT CAN SURVIVE SHOCKS

This is not about producing more at any cost. It is about securing more of what we already produce.

THE REAL MEASURE OF PROGRESS

Perhaps Tamil Nadu should eventually publish a Food Continuity Index alongside agricultural production statistics. Not merely: How many tonnes did we produce? But:

  • How many tonnes reached people?
  • How much was lost?
  • How many days of strategic food reserves do we have?
  • How much storage capacity exists near production centres?
  • How much surplus can be processed?
  • How much irrigation depends on stressed groundwater?
  • How much fallow land can responsibly be restored?
  • How much of our strategic food requirement is domestically secured?
  • How quickly can the system recover after a climate shock?

These measurements would tell us something that production statistics alone cannot.

THE CONTINUITY TEST

We cannot stop every drought. We cannot prevent every flood. We cannot control every monsoon. We cannot guarantee that every harvest will succeed. But we can decide whether one failed harvest becomes a food crisis.

ONE FARM MAY FAIL.
ONE CROP MAY FAIL.
ONE DISTRICT MAY FAIL.
THE FOOD SYSTEM MUST CONTINUE.

That is the meaning of food resilience. And that is where Tamil Nadu’s next agricultural mission could begin.

NOT SIMPLY TO GROW MORE FOOD. BUT TO SECURE MORE OF THE FOOD WE GROW.

BE FOOD-SECURE TODAY. BE FOOD-RESILIENT TOMORROW. ENSURE FOOD CONTINUITY FOR THE GENERATIONS AFTER US.

The harvest of today can become the food security of tomorrow.
